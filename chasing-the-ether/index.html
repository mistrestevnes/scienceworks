Chasing the Ether

By the 1800s, experiments had convinced physicists that light is a wave. Every wave they knew of traveled through something, so they decided space must be filled with an invisible substance for light to wave in: the ether. In 1887, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley built the most sensitive measuring machine of its day to detect Earth moving through it. They found nothing, and that nothing became one of the most important results in physics.

In 1704, Isaac Newton argued that light is a stream of tiny particles. Because of Newton's huge reputation, most scientists agreed with him for the next hundred years.

Between 1801 and 1803, Thomas Young tested the idea. He split a narrow beam of light in two, using a thin card or two narrow slits, and looked at where the light landed on a screen. Particles should go straight through and make two bright stripes. Waves should spread out from each opening and overlap.

A spot that shouldn't exist

In 1818, Augustin Fresnel entered a wave theory of light in a French Academy contest. Siméon Poisson, a particle supporter, pointed out that Fresnel's math predicted a bright spot in the middle of the shadow of a round disk. He thought that was absurd. In 1819, François Arago tried it and saw the spot.

The race through water

Newton's particle theory predicted light moves faster in water than in air. Wave theory predicted slower. In 1850, Léon Foucault measured both. Light was slower in water, and most physicists considered the particle theory finished.

Every wave anyone had studied traveled through a material. Ocean waves move through water, and sound moves through air. The wave is the material jiggling, so no material means no wave.

In 1660, Robert Boyle put a ringing bell inside a glass jar and pumped the air out. Try it. Put a lamp in there too.

Sunlight crosses about 150 million km of nearly empty space and reaches us in about 8 minutes. If light is a wave, something has to be doing the waving out there.

Physicists named that something the luminiferous ether, which means "light-carrying ether." It was never seen or touched. It was required because the rest of the picture seemed to demand it, and it needed some strange properties.

In 1865, James Clerk Maxwell showed that light is a wave of electricity and magnetism and calculated its speed: about 300,000 km/s. That raised a question: 300,000 km/s measured relative to what? Most physicists, Maxwell included, answered that it was relative to the ether.

Everywhere

The ether had to fill all of space, including a jar with the air pumped out, since light crosses both.

Stiffer than steel

Light is about 50,000 times faster than sound in steel. Faster waves need a stiffer material, so the ether had to be incredibly rigid.

Like a solid

Experiments with polarized light in the 1810s and 1820s showed light waves shake sideways. Sideways waves can travel through the inside of a solid, but not through the inside of a gas or liquid.

No drag

Planets plow through it with no sign of slowing down. So the ether had to be a rigid solid that planets pass through without any friction.

If the ether sits still and Earth moves through it at 30 km/s, then on Earth we should feel an ether wind, the way you feel wind riding a bike on a calm day. The next station shows how you might detect it.

If light is a wave in the ether, it moves through the ether the way a boat moves through water. Earth orbits the Sun at 30 km/s, so we should be plowing through the ether, with an "ether wind" streaming past us.

Michelson's plan was to race two light beams, one along that wind and one across it. Try the same race with boats first. Both boats swim at 2.0 m/s through the water, out 60 m to a buoy and back.

Station 4

A race track for light

Drag the stone to turn it.

A half-silvered mirror in the middle splits one beam of light in two. One half runs down arm 1, the other down arm 2. Mirrors send both halves back, and they meet again in the telescope.

If one half arrives even slightly late, the light waves fall out of step and the striped pattern in the telescope, called interference fringes, slides sideways. Turning the stone swaps which arm points into the ether wind, so the stripes should slide back and forth.

The time gap is in femtoseconds: millionths of a billionth of a second. The real arms used extra mirrors to bounce the light back and forth, fitting 11 m of path onto a stone slab 1.5 m across. The slab floated on a pool of mercury so it could turn smoothly without shaking.

From April to July 1887, Michelson and Morley worked in the basement of a dormitory at Western Reserve University in Cleveland. They turned the floating stone slowly and read the fringes against a crosshair at marks around the trough.

With 11 m arms and Earth moving at 30 km/s, the ether theory says the fringes should swing about 0.44 of a fringe between 0° and 90°, twice per full turn. That is easy to see. Now take the readings yourself.

Explaining nothing

Boat B · across—

Michelson and Morley's machine worked, yet the ether wind never showed up. Physicists looked for a rescue. In 1889 George FitzGerald, and in 1892 Hendrik Lorentz on his own, proposed that anything moving through the ether gets squeezed along its direction of motion by a factor of

√(1 − v²/c²)

That is exactly the squeeze needed to make the river race from Station 3 a tie. Here course A is shortened by that factor, using the boat's speed for c and the current for v.

In the ether picture, Earth at 30 km/s would be squeezed by about 5 parts per billion. Across Earth's 12,742 km diameter, that is about 6 cm. No ruler could catch it, because the ruler would shrink too.

The fix worked, but it looked like a patch made to fit one result. In 1905 Einstein started from the other end. He took as a starting principle that light travels at the same speed for every observer, and that no experiment can detect absolute motion. Then there is no ether wind to find.

The squeeze comes back in Einstein's theory too, as length contraction, and the same factor appears in time dilation. Those are the next modules.

Modern versions of this experiment use lasers and rotating optical cavities. A 2009 Berlin experiment (Herrmann and colleagues) found light's speed the same in every direction to about 1 part in 1017.
